How to read this map

The colours are quantiles. Boroughs are sorted and divided into groups of equal size, so each shade holds roughly the same number of boroughs. Equal steps in colour are not equal steps in value. The strip above shows where each borough sits and where the class boundaries fall.

Grey means not reported. Six boroughs did not submit at least one quarterly return in the year to March 2026; Ealing submitted one of four.

Each measure says whether it is official statistics or an estimate. Household counts, length of stay, children and placements outside the borough are MHCLG statutory returns. Rates are estimates derived from each council's published payment data and have not been confirmed against an invoice.

A blank rate means no usable figure. Where a council's single payments cover several units, no per-night rate can be derived, and none is shown. The reason is given in the tooltip and in the borough's own figures.

Why this is a shared problem

Capital Letters: the pan-London vehicle that existed

Between 2019 and 2025 London's boroughs ran a shared company for procuring private rented accommodation for homeless households. Capital Letters was established in December 2018, had thirteen member boroughs by early 2019 and began operating in September 2019, with around £38m of MHCLG funding. It came out of a 2017 feasibility study commissioned by the boroughs, which made five recommendations.

What it did. It implemented three of the five: a central procurement team, a London-branded offer to landlords, and an allocations platform. LSE London's commissioned review records about 6,500 properties procured and 4,500 let to borough nominees; 67% of families housed in their own borough, against 41% across London under the boroughs' inter-borough agreement; a pan-London agreement on landlord incentive payments that reduced competition between boroughs; and a tenancy sustainment service. It closed in 2025. Secondary reporting on the MHCLG post-mortem attributes the closure to boroughs' unwillingness to transfer staff and to rising rents.

What it did not do. The study's first recommendation was that the company become the tenants' landlord. The housing benefit subsidy a council can claim for temporary accommodation it holds is capped at 90% of the January 2011 Local Housing Allowance; a private tenancy claims the current allowance. If a separate body is the landlord, the cap does not apply, and the 2017 study put the saving at up to £37m a year across London on the rates of that year. Transferring leases and landlord status proved harder than assumed, and most boroughs wanted to keep control of their leased stock, so this was set aside rather than tested. The 2011 baseline is now fifteen years old and the allowance has been uprated twice since (2020 and 2024) before being frozen again, so the gap is larger than the 2017 figure; it has not been recomputed. Since 1 May 2026 the Renters' Rights Act 2025 has also let a landlord recover a property used as temporary accommodation once the council no longer needs it (ground 5G).

Sources: LSE London, Capital Letters: A Review, commissioned evaluation; Tim Gray Consultancy, feasibility study of options for pan-London collaboration in procuring private rented accommodation, September 2017; Housing Today on the MHCLG review of the company's closure. Method and confidence

Household counts, length of stay, children and placements outside the borough are MHCLG statutory returns: official statistics, not modelled. Length of stay is the time already spent by households present at the quarter end, not the length of a completed stay. Rates are estimates derived from councils' published payment files and have not been confirmed against an invoice.

What this map does not show, and why

It does not rank boroughs on published spend. Each borough's published file captures a different and unknowable share of its bill. Published spend per household-night ranges from £3 to £107 across London, which measures publication practice rather than procurement. What can be concluded: a borough's own total over its own period. What cannot: which borough spends more.

It does not compare an operator's price between boroughs unless both payments sit under the same homelessness label. Without that, one firm's payments mix homelessness lets with children's semi-independent placements, which are different products at different prices.

It does not show a rate where single payments cover several units. Such a payment cannot be divided into a per-night rate.
